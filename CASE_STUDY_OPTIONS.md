# Candidate CRSS case studies for Chapter 2

CRSS contains police-reported crashes rather than insurance policies or
exposure. Every proposed case study therefore estimates an outcome **conditional
on a crash having occurred**. None estimates accident frequency, policy risk, or
the probability that a journey results in a crash.

## Option 1: Injury to a particular occupant

### Question

> Given a crash and the characteristics of a particular driver or passenger,
> what is the probability that the person is injured?

### Dataset and observation

This option uses `dt_crss_inj_occupant`, with one row per driver or passenger.
People are nested within vehicles, and vehicles are nested within crashes. The
unique key is `case_number + vehicle_number + person_number`.

The binary target is `injured`:

- 0: no apparent injury;
- 1: possible, minor, serious, fatal, or severity-unknown injury.

There are 120,475 occupants. Excluding three people coded as having died before
the crash, 33,407 of 120,472 occupants were injured (**27.73%** of the CRSS
sample).

### Potential predictors

- age and sex;
- driver or passenger status;
- seating position and restraint use;
- vehicle age, type, construction, and safety equipment;
- road, junction, lighting, weather, and traffic-control conditions;
- optionally, police-reconstructed pre-impact conduct such as distraction,
  impairment, speed, and avoidance behaviour.

### Exclusions

- vehicle and crash injury summaries;
- hospitalisation and other direct outcome information;
- impact and damage variables if the model is presented as pre-crash;
- occupants from the same crash must not be divided between training and test
  data.

### Insurance interpretation

This resembles bodily-injury severity assessment for a specific vehicle
occupant. It naturally demonstrates interactions involving age, seat position,
restraint use, and vehicle characteristics. It also provides a later bridge to
multilevel models because occupants share vehicles and crashes.

### Main limitation

It is a person-level model with clustered observations. An ordinary decision
tree does not model the vehicle/crash random effects explicitly, although
grouped validation by crash prevents direct train/test leakage.

## Option 2: Crash involving an injured pedestrian

### Question

> Given a police-reported crash, what is the probability that at least one
> pedestrian was injured?

### Dataset and observation

This option would require a dedicated table with one row per crash and
`injured_pedestrian` as the binary target. The repository deliberately does not
publish a general-purpose crash table containing mixed-timing fields.

Of 51,658 crashes, 2,663 involved at least one injured pedestrian (**5.15%** of
the CRSS sample).

### Potential predictors

- urban or rural location;
- road, junction, intersection, and speed-limit characteristics;
- time of day, lighting, and weather;
- work-zone, school-zone, and bus-stop context;
- types and number of vehicles;
- driver and vehicle circumstances that do not directly reveal pedestrian
  involvement.

### Exclusions

- number of pedestrians or non-motorists in the crash;
- pedestrian and bicycle crash-type files;
- person-level pedestrian records;
- event descriptions that state that a pedestrian or non-occupant was struck;
- pedestrian injury counts or severity fields.

### Insurance interpretation

This represents vulnerable-road-user bodily-injury exposure. It provides a
clear rare-event classification problem and makes class imbalance, evaluation
metrics, and asymmetric error costs relevant.

### Main limitation

Almost every pedestrian represented in CRSS was injured. The target therefore
mainly distinguishes crashes involving a pedestrian from other crashes rather
than distinguishing injury from non-injury among pedestrians.

## Option 3: Serious or fatal injury from journey-start information

### Question

> Given the people, vehicle, journey, road, and environmental conditions known
> before the collision, and conditional on a crash occurring, what is the
> probability that at least one person is seriously or fatally injured?

### Dataset and observation

This option uses the dedicated `dt_crss_sev_crash` table, with one
row per crash and `serious_or_fatal_injury` as the binary target. The table is
physically restricted to eligible fields rather than requiring readers to
remove post-crash columns during modelling.

Of 51,658 crashes, 6,662 resulted in at least one serious or fatal injury
(**12.90%** of the CRSS sample).

### Restricted predictor set

The principal model uses only characteristics and context that could have been
answered before the crash:

- occupant composition where available before the journey;
- vehicle age, type, construction, and installed safety equipment;
- road type, route, junction, speed limit, and traffic control;
- time, lighting, and weather;
- vehicle defects and existing vision obstructions.

It excludes information generated by the collision:

- impact point, collision manner, rollover, and event sequence;
- damage, towing, airbag deployment, and ejection;
- injury outcomes and summaries;
- identifiers, survey variables, and folds as predictors.

It also excludes crash composition:

- number of vehicles involved.

Reported travel speed, speeding, alcohol involvement, driver factors,
distraction, impairment, avoidance manoeuvres, charged violations, and vehicle
special factors are retained as optional retrospective predictors. They
describe conduct or status that existed before impact, but were recorded or
determined after the crash. Their timing is labelled in the accompanying schema
so an analysis can include or omit them.

### Optional retrospective extension

An explicitly labelled retrospective model may add police-reconstructed
pre-impact conduct:

- distraction and mobile-phone use;
- impairment and fatigue;
- speeding;
- avoidance behaviour.

These fields are present in `dt_crss_sev_crash` and explicitly marked
as retrospective. A principal model can omit them using the schema, while an
expanded model can include them to show the value gained from information that
was unavailable at journey start.

### Insurance interpretation

This is a conditional crash-severity model rather than an accident-frequency
model. It asks how personal, vehicle, route, and environmental conditions alter
the severity distribution once a crash occurs. The 12.90% event rate is large
enough for a stable introductory classification-tree analysis while retaining a
meaningful minority class.

### Main limitation

Some information, particularly seat-belt use and exact route context, existed
before the crash but would not necessarily have been held by an insurer. The
case study must distinguish “defined before impact” from “available in an
operational insurance system.”

## Comparison

| Option | Grain | Target rate | Principal teaching value | Main complication |
|---|---|---:|---|---|
| Particular occupant injured | Person | 27.73% | Individual heterogeneity, interactions, and hierarchical data | People are clustered within vehicles and crashes |
| Injured pedestrian | Crash | 5.15% | Rare events, class imbalance, and vulnerable-road-user exposure | Mostly identifies pedestrian involvement |
| Serious/fatal injury from journey-start information | Crash | 12.90% | Clean information timing and conditional severity | Pre-impact information is not always insurer-held information |

## Recommended Chapter 2 choice

The journey-start serious/fatal-injury case study provides the cleanest primary
example. It has one row per crash, a meaningful binary target, a clear insurance
severity interpretation, and an explicit feature-availability boundary. The
occupant and pedestrian studies remain useful extensions demonstrating
hierarchical observations and rare-event classification, respectively.
